Sitka: is it sound, what it costs, how to start

Deep research on github.com/nicedreamzapp/sitka · November 18, 2026 · prices marked "EST" are estimates, not quotes

The short answer: the sitting physics works. Sitka can hold you in a chair pose: the struts carry 70% of your weight and it has 9° of margin before tipping back. A second, deeper pass checked every number against the CAD and confirmed every flaw in the current parts and found a better path. The new plan: the hybrid "Sitka Seat"

For each problem, the simplest proven everyday part that already solves it:

  • Knee lock → a locking gas spring across the knee, like the ones in office chairs and hospital beds, released by a lever on the belt. It locks at any angle. If something slips you sink slowly instead of dropping. That removes the knee arc, yoke, clutch and 3.6 kN proof chain entirely. Noonee's Chairless Chair works the same way. Backup to test on the same bench: a $35 BMX freewheel ratchet.
  • Spring → latex speargun band. 22–30 g per knee and about $8 stores the 44 J. The glass leaf can only reach 13–40% of that.
  • Strut → crutch lower tubes with the push-button pin and rubber tip. They're rated for 300 lb users and cost $35–41 a pair. Sitka only needs 3 lengths.
  • Leg frame → a hinged post-op knee brace ($41–110) for the thigh cuff, shin cuff, hinge and straps, all bought.
  • Seat → a wide bicycle saddle shell per side. Bike saddles already solved sit-bone loading. The saddle rails become the strut hinge, so no printed part carries your weight.
  • Sit-bone cushion → a two-cell air insert (ROHO style) under each sit bone, plus a cup that reaches back under the thigh so soft tissue takes about 40%. Air beats gel in the wheelchair research. Today's peak is about 86 kPa; the target is under 45 kPa for 20 minutes.
  • Belt → a pack hip belt with a 2 mm plastic framesheet, so the cable guides stop sitting on soft fabric.
  • Backrest → Crazy Creek straps, unchanged. Tension before structure.

The 3D printer now makes the brackets, jigs, pads and test dummy. It is no longer in the load path, so a print failure can't drop you.

Printer: own one or use a service?

  • Buy a Bambu P2S (~$449 on the current Black Friday price, through Dec 1) for brackets, jigs, the TPU pads and the test dummy. Add a hardened nozzle and a dryer to print carbon nylon.
  • Send the big shells out to MJF nylon (JLC3DP, about $0.28/g). That's about $26 per seat cup and $81 per thigh shell. MJF nylon is equally strong in every direction, while a home print is weak between layers, and that matters for anything you sit on.
  • Step up to an H2S ($1,049, 340 mm bed) only if the shells go through about 5 revisions, since that money buys about 13 sets of MJF shells.
  • Filament: PETG ~$20/kg, TPU 95A ~$40/kg, carbon nylon (PA6-CF / PAHT-CF) ~$43–100/kg.

Below: the first audit of the design as it stands in the repo today. The hybrid above replaces most of the expensive metal in it.

1. Is it sound? Module by module

  • Sound: the seat physics, the carbon strut's strength (holds about 9x the load), the spine links, the hip spring, the bands, the lift physics, and the shoulder helper.
  • Fix: springs. The glass leaf springs can store only about 18 J of the 44 J the sit needs. Swap in latex speargun bands: about 30 g and $10 a knee, with an almost flat pull (the same thing HeroWear does).
  • Fix: knee arc. It hangs off one point and would break if a strut slipped (the 3.6 kN catch). Bolt both ends back to the hinge with laser-cut side plates. That makes it stronger and about a third of the price.
  • Fix: clutch. The 47 mm one-way clutch does not fit its 44 mm housing. Put the spool on the clutch shaft and grow the housing to about 60 × 30 mm. Add the missing rewind spring. A $20 generic clutch is fine for testing.
  • Fix: strut locks. Twist locks slip under steady weight. Use crutch push-button locks at two set lengths. Crutches already hold full body weight this way.
  • Fix: seat cup. At 3.5 mm thick it would slowly creep. Make it 5–6 mm with ribs around the fork, print it bowl-down, and hold 1.5x your weight for 24 hours.
  • Fix: belt. The cable guides sit on soft belt fabric and would roll it. Add a stiff strip from hip to hip.
  • Not ready: the ankle has no frame in the CAD yet. The smart clutch is untested research. The motor works on paper, so build it last.

2. Will it go together?

  • The CAD checks that parts touch, but it has no bolt holes, inserts or backing plates yet. Add them before printing load parts.
  • Four parts are too big for a 256 mm printer bed (thigh shell, back panel and two pads). Split them away from the bolt zones, or print on a larger bed (H2S/H2D).
  • The fit report flags small clashes: the knee arc sits 3.7 mm into the knee, the seat cups pinch at the center when you sit, and the knee line can slip off the arc when running. All are small edits.
  • The main shells have no STL files in the repo yet. Export pre-split STLs so anyone can print them.

3. Top 10 improvements (biggest payoff for the least work first)

  1. Latex bands for the sit and lift springs
  2. Knee arc bolted at both ends with laser-cut plates
  3. Crutch push-button strut locks
  4. Stiff strip in the belt
  5. Thicker ribbed seat cup and a 24-hour creep test
  6. Clutch housing redrawn around the real clutch, with a rewind spring and a generic clutch
  7. A DIY pressure mat (about $40) to measure the seat cushion
  8. Bolt holes and backing plates in the CAD
  9. Clear the fit-report clashes
  10. Pre-split STLs in the repo, and drop the parts nothing uses yet

4. Why it costs what it does

  • Printed parts are cheap: about $230 for the whole starter. A part costs its filament weight × about $0.10/g for carbon nylon or $0.04/g for TPU, plus 15% waste. The thigh shell (626 g) is the biggest at $68.
  • One-off machined metal is half the bill ($1,415). A machine shop charges setup and programming time, and for a single part that setup is most of the price. That's why the knee arc is $320 and the hinges $280. Laser-cut plate from SendCutSend runs about $13 each for one, or $5 each for ten.
  • Bought parts, $1,205: the branded clutches alone are $300. Generic ones are about $20 each.
  • The five biggest lines are 43% of the cost: arc and yoke $320, branded clutches $300, machined hinges $280, struts $190, spools $140. Fixing those five gets the starter to about $1,850.

Where each idea came from

  • Collins unpowered ankle (Nature 2015): a spring and clutch with no motor cut walking effort 7%. This is the proof behind Sitka's springs-first idea.
  • Noonee Chairless Chair ($2,190–2,890): it locks at any knee angle. Sitka's one-way clutch already does this. Borrow their quick release.
  • Archelis wearable chair: a shin-and-thigh shell that becomes a seat. Borrow how it folds flat.
  • HeroWear Apex / Paexo Back: rubber or spring lift assist with an on/off switch. That's the exact model for the latex lift module.
  • Hypershell / Dnsys / Ascentiz ($599–1,999): powered hip units. Borrow the clip-on modules, the app and the packaging. They prove people pay $600–1,200.
  • Skip / Arc'teryx MO/GO ($4,500–5,000): the motor built into the pants. That's the long-term look for Sitka's one-motor tier.
  • Crazy Creek camp chair: straps instead of a rigid frame. Already Sitka's backrest, and the rule for every new part: tension before structure.
  • Crutches and trekking poles: proven push-button locks and carbon tubes, so we buy instead of invent.
  • Bicycle freehubs: sealed, cheap, proven one-way clutches. A good backup if the sprag clutch gives trouble.

Business plan, starting small

  1. Week 1, bench (~$500 + printer): build the step-0 rig and film each test passing. That footage is the first real content.
  2. Wear it: put the passing parts on two braces with a belt and saddles, and film sitting, standing and walking. Kickstarter requires a working prototype and bans photoreal renders, so this video is the campaign.
  3. First product: "Sitka Seat", the passive sit-only unit (two legs plus the belt, no electronics). Price it at $599 retail and $499 early bird on a $150–200 parts cost at 100 units. That's the 3–4x margin hardware needs. It sits between LEX ($297, a perch; raised $143K) and Noonee ($2,890, a true sit).
  4. Second product: an open-source printed-shell kit at about $299 for makers. It's only a second offer, because we can't control how people assemble it.
  5. Kickstarter: fees are about 9% all-in. Comps used small goals and raised $143K–2.5M. No wearable seat that truly sits has ever run there.
  6. Safety and legal: a 120 kg user limit on the label, a fall warning, and a proof-test record for every unit. Make no medical claims, so it stays a CPSC consumer product and not FDA. Product liability insurance runs about $1,000–5,000 a year at the start. Follow ASTM F48: F3358 labeling, F3795 risk management, F3474 ergonomics, F3517 movement tests, F3444 user training.
  7. Upgrades later: lift module (latex back bands), walking bands, then the smart clutch and the one motor. These come from the original Sitka plan, once the seat sells.

What we depend on

  • Bambu printer and filament: any enclosed printer that can run carbon nylon works. The files are open, so we're not locked in.
  • SendCutSend / JLCCNC for metal: any laser or waterjet shop can cut the same flat plates. The long-term plan is our own small fiber laser or CNC.
  • CubeMars motor (step 6 only): swappable for any quasi-direct-drive motor. We can build our own from an open design later.
