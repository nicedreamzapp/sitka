Sitkaskeleton

Concept v2, October 21, 2026. Motorized knees that help you walk, hike and run, then lock at the angle you pick so you can sit anywhere. Numbers on this page come from the models in this folder.

Side view, chair mode (drawn to scale)

How it works

A small robot-joint motor sits at each knee. While you walk, hike or run it adds torque as your knee works, the way Dnsys and Hypershell units help today.

When you stop, it offers you a seat. Tap once and the motors bend your knees to the angle you picked, a steel pawl drops into a toothed ring, and a pad folds under each thigh. Your weight goes through the locked frame into your heels.

The pawl holds the seat, not the motor. A chair-mode hold is about 142 Nm per knee, three times the motor's peak, and the pawl does it for zero watts.

Hard safety rule: the lock only engages when both feet and the motion sensor say you're standing still. Never at a walk, never at a run.

Four lock angles

Figures are drawn from the same geometry as the CAD: shank leaned forward until the seat sits over the heel (capped at 35°). The amber number is the knee torque the lock carries for a 130 kg, 95th-percentile man, balanced and static.

New things nobody sells yet

Seat offer. It notices you've stopped for a second or two and gives a soft buzz. One tap and you're sitting.

Downhill braking. On descents and stairs going down, the motors brake your knees so your quads work less. It also recovers a little charge, though only a few watts (see below).

Fall catch. If it senses a stumble, both knees go to full torque and heavy damping in a few milliseconds. That buys time; it cannot hold a falling adult by itself.

Place memory. It remembers the angle you like at each spot, like the shop bench or the lift line, and suggests it.
